Which river is actually the longest — the Nile or the Amazon?

The Nile is the textbook answer. But every published measurement of the Amazon's length overlaps with — and often exceeds — every published measurement of the Nile's.

Every commonly published Nile figure falls inside the range of published Amazon figures, so "longer" depends on which legitimate measurement you pick. Two judgment calls decide a river's length: where it ends, and which headwater stream counts as its source.

Many numbers that sound measured are really a definition plus a measurement. When two credible sources disagree, ask whether they're measuring the same thing, from the same starting point to the same end.

What “settled” ranking is really a choice about where to start and stop?

Why there's no clean answer

Every commonly published Nile figure falls inside the range of published Amazon figures — so "longer" depends entirely on which legitimate measurement you pick, not on a settled fact. A river doesn't come with a factory-installed length. Two judgment calls decide the number, and reasonable experts have made different calls on both. Where does it end? The Amazon empties into the Atlantic through a mouth so wide, split into multiple channels around large islands, that there's no single point where "river" clearly becomes "ocean." Pick a different outlet channel and the length changes.

Where does it begin? A river's "source" is whichever headwater stream you decide is the true beginning — a judgment call, not a fact you can look up. A 2007 Brazilian-led expedition traced a new Amazon source to a snow-capped peak in southern Peru, instantly adding about 176 miles (284 km) to the total.
