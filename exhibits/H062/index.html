Which river is actually the longest — the Nile or the Amazon?

The Nile is the textbook answer. But every published measurement of the Amazon's length overlaps with — and often exceeds — every published measurement of the Nile's.

Why there's no clean answer

A river doesn't come with a factory-installed length. Two judgment calls decide the number, and reasonable experts have made different calls on both. Where does it end? The Amazon empties into the Atlantic through a mouth so wide — dozens of kilometers across, split into multiple channels — that there's no single point where "river" clearly becomes "ocean." Pick a different outlet channel and the length changes.

Where does it begin? A river's "source" is whichever headwater stream you decide is the true beginning — a judgment call, not a fact you can look up. A 2014 Brazilian-led expedition traced a new Amazon source to a snow-capped peak in southern Peru, instantly adding hundreds of kilometers to the total.

Every commonly published Nile figure falls inside the range of published Amazon figures — so "longer" depends entirely on which legitimate measurement you pick, not on a settled fact.

If two rivers can't agree on where they start or stop, how many other "settled" rankings are really just a choice of definition?

Sources: Encyclopaedia Britannica, "How Long Is the Amazon River?"; National Geographic, "Amazon Longer Than Nile River, Scientists Say."
