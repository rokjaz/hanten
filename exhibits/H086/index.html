The unemployment rate is 4.1%. Does that mean almost everyone who wants a job has one?

The official number is reported correctly. But "unemployed" has a precise official definition that is narrower than the everyday word, and the government publishes six different measures for the same month.

One month, three true answers (U.S., August 2026)

1.8%

The official rate
4.1%

Broadest measure
7.7%

Six official measures of "not working enough," same month

Each measure from U-4 to U-6 counts everyone in the one above it, plus a new group.

A number is only as broad as its definition. To count as officially unemployed, you need to have no job and to have looked for one in the past four weeks. Stop looking and you are no longer unemployed, officially. Work 10 hours a week when you want 40 and you are employed. Nothing about the 4.1% is false; it just measures something narrower than the word suggests.

The same gap shows up with poverty, homelessness, crime, "on time" flights and "graduation" rates: each has an official definition that decides who gets counted. Before comparing two numbers, or reacting to one, check what it actually counts.

Try it somewhere else

The U.S. Department of Transportation counts a flight as on time if it arrives less than 15 minutes late.

All six are seasonally adjusted. People who don't want a job, like most retirees and full-time students, aren't counted in any of them.

So is the official rate misleading?

Not by itself. The official rate is carefully defined, measured the same way every month, and useful for tracking changes over time. The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes all six measures together every month. The problem comes when a narrow official number gets described with a broad everyday word, like "everyone who wants a job has one," or when two numbers with different definitions are compared as if they meant the same thing.
