Life expectancy in 1900 was under 48. Did people back then really die at 40?

The number is real. But life expectancy at birth averages every death together, including the many babies and young children who died in 1900. Most adults lived far longer than the average suggests.

An average can describe almost nobody. In 1900, deaths clustered at two ends of life: in early childhood and in old age. Averaging a baby who died at 1 with a grandmother who died at 75 gives 38, an age at which neither of them died. Once a child made it past the dangerous early years, reaching 60 or 70 was common.

The same trap works anywhere a few extreme values sit far from the rest: average income pulled up by a few billionaires, an average commute pulled up by a few long ones. Before trusting what an average says about a typical person, ask how spread out the numbers behind it are.

The chart uses the average of the men's and women's figures. These are "period" numbers: they describe the death rates of 1900 as if a person lived their whole life under them. People born in 1900 actually lived longer, because medicine kept improving during their lives. The government's official figure for 1900, based on the 10 states that recorded deaths then, is 47.3.

So what made life expectancy rise so much?

Mostly, fewer children died. Clean water, sewers, pasteurized milk, vaccines and later antibiotics cut deaths from infections that hit babies and young children hardest. Saving a baby adds 70 or more years to the average; adding a year to an 80-year-old's life adds just one. That's why life expectancy at birth rose by about 30 years over the 20th century, while the years still expected at age 65 grew by only about 6.
