Which century years get a February 29?

The Gregorian calendar, adopted in 1582, drops three leap days every 400 years

A leap day every four years overshoots, so the calendar quietly skips three of them every 400 years. A year is about 365.2422 days, not 365.25. Adding a day every four years makes the calendar run about 11 minutes long each year, and those minutes pile up to a full day roughly every 128 years. The fix: century years are leap years only if they divide evenly by 400. So 2000 was a leap year, but 1700, 1800, 1900, and 2100 are not.

Simple rules people repeat are often the first step of a longer rule. Before relying on one, ask whether it has exceptions that only show up rarely, like once a century.

Why the calendar had to be fixed in 1582

The older Julian calendar used the simple every-four-years rule. By the 1500s it had drifted about 10 days away from the seasons, pushing the spring equinox, and the date of Easter that depends on it, earlier and earlier. Pope Gregory XIII's 1582 reform jumped the calendar ahead 10 days (October 4 was followed by October 15) and added the century rule. Britain and its colonies did not switch until 1752, when they had to skip 11 days.
