The election version — four times the popular-vote winner lost

Hits don’t win games and votes don’t win presidencies; runs and states do. A hit only becomes a run if it’s bunched with other hits or comes with runners already on base, so a team can scatter 12 hits across nine innings and score twice. A popular vote only produces electoral votes if it helps win a state; a landslide in a safe state adds to the national total without moving a single electoral vote.

Whenever a contest is decided by winning units (runs, states, districts) rather than by adding everything up, the total and the winner can point in different directions. Before trusting a total, check what the scoreboard actually counts.

What are you counting, and is it what decides the outcome?

How did 12 hits turn into only 2 runs?

The visitors spread their hits out. They had at least one hit in every inning, but never more than two. A single with two outs, or a single followed by a strikeout and a double play, leaves the runner stranded. Their only runs came from a single followed by a double in the 3rd and a solo home run in the 7th.

Home bunched theirs. In the 4th inning a walk and three straight hits, the last a two-run double, produced three runs. A solo home run in the 7th made it 4–2. Their other hit, in the 8th, didn’t score.

This game is made up, but the pattern is common: a hit is a chance to score, not a run. Walks, extra-base hits, outs, and above all the order in which things happen decide how many runs a team gets from its hits.

1876 is the only case where the popular-vote winner had an outright majority (over 50%), not just a plurality — Samuel Tilden remains the only candidate in American history to lose the presidency after winning a majority of the popular vote. A fifth election, 1824, is often mentioned alongside these: Andrew Jackson won both the popular vote and the most electoral votes, but fell short of a majority, and the House of Representatives chose John Quincy Adams instead — a different mechanism than the other four, so it's kept separate here.
