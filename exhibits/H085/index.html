What's really connecting them

When two things move together, something else may be moving both of them. Ice cream doesn't make anyone drown, and drownings don't sell ice cream. Hot weather does both: it sends people to the freezer aisle and to the pool, lake and beach.

This hidden-third-factor trap shows up everywhere. Kids with bigger shoes read better, because older kids have bigger feet and more practice reading. Countries that eat more chocolate win more Nobel Prizes, and both go with being wealthy. When two trends line up, ask what else could be driving both before believing that one causes the other.

How can you tell if one thing really causes another?

The strongest test is an experiment: randomly split people into two groups, change one thing for only one group, and see whether the outcome changes. Random assignment spreads hidden factors like weather, age or wealth evenly across both groups, so they can't explain the difference. When experiments aren't possible, researchers compare cases where the suspected hidden factor is the same, such as comparing ice cream sales and drownings only on days with the same temperature. If the link disappears, the hidden factor was doing the work.
