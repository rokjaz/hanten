How do you catch a liar when there's no evidence?

A king, medieval priests, and a rock band each faced a claim they couldn't check. None of them asked a better question. Each designed a test that would cost the liar and the truth-teller differently, then watched who flinched.

Ancient Israel · 1 Kings 3

King Solomon and the two mothers

Two women who share a house each gave birth days apart. One baby died in the night. Both now claim the living child, and no one else saw either birth.

The test

Solomon calls for a sword and orders the child cut in two, half for each woman.

The real motherBegs him to give the baby to the other woman rather than see it killed.
The impostorLoses nothing real either way, so she agrees: divide it.

Solomon gives the child to the woman who tried to give him up.

Medieval Europe · 9th–13th centuries

Trial by ordeal

An accused person with no witnesses could be sent to "God's judgment": carry a red-hot iron or plunge a hand into boiling water. If the wound healed cleanly, God had declared them innocent.

The test

Let the accused choose: undergo the ordeal, or confess and settle.

The innocentBelieved God would protect them, so they took the ordeal.
The guiltyBelieved God would expose them, so many confessed or settled first.
100 ended before the ordeal 130 passed 78 failed

In 308 cases recorded at Várad, Hungary (1208–1235), 62.5% of those who went through the hot-iron ordeal passed. Priests knew who had chosen it, and could quietly make sure the iron wasn't that hot.

U.S. concert tours · 1980s

Van Halen's brown M&Ms

Van Halen's stage show was heavy, high-voltage, and full of pyrotechnics. Every venue promised it had read and followed the band's long technical contract. There was no way to check every line.

The test

Bury one trivial line in the contract: M&Ms backstage, "absolutely no brown ones."

A careful venueRead every line, so the bowl had no brown M&Ms.
A careless venueSkimmed the contract, and the brown M&Ms gave it away.

Finding a brown M&M meant the crew rechecked the whole setup. At one Colorado arena that skipped the specs, the stage sank into the floor, causing about $80,000 in damage.

A question can be answered with a lie. A well-designed test can't be faked for free. Each test works because the truth-teller and the liar would react differently to the same situation: a real mother can't bear to see her child killed, an innocent person who believes in divine judgment has nothing to fear from it, and a venue that actually read the contract has nothing to hide.

The same idea runs through modern life: a warranty signals a maker who trusts its product, a security deposit filters out tenants who plan to skip out, and a spot check on one small detail tells you whether someone paid attention to the big ones.

What small, checkable detail would show whether someone got the big thing right?

What makes a test like this work

It has to cost the liar more than the truth-teller. Asking both women to describe the baby wouldn't work, because they had shared a house for days. Asking both to pay for the baby wouldn't work either; it would only reveal who had more money. The sword worked because the price was different for each of them.

People have to believe in it. The ordeal only sorted the guilty from the innocent because nearly everyone believed God would reveal the truth. After the Fourth Lateran Council barred priests from taking part in 1215, the practice faded, and so did the trick.

And the reason for the test can look absurd from outside. For years the brown M&M rule was retold as proof of rock-star excess. It was actually a safety check.
