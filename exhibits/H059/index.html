How much more does a Wyoming vote count than a Texas vote?

Every state gets at least 3 electoral votes no matter how few people live there. That floor means small states end up with far more electoral power per resident than large ones — a real, measurable, and permanent gap, mapped here across every state.

The floor of 3 electoral votes does the work

Every state gets 2 electoral votes for its senators and at least 1 for a House seat, regardless of population — that floor is what gives Wyoming, Vermont, and the other smallest states outsized weight per resident. Large states don't get a matching ceiling: their electoral votes grow with population, but total electors are capped at 538, so growth gets spread thin.

Congressional apportionment (and electoral votes with it) is also only recalculated once per decade, on the 2020 census. States that have grown quickly since then — Texas among them — end up under-represented relative to their current population even before the small-state floor effect is considered.

The gap between the most and least "weighted" vote is roughly 4-to-1 — and it's built into the Constitution's math, not an accident of any particular election.

A representation system with a floor and a cap will always favor whoever sits closest to the floor — who does that describe here?
