How much more does a Wyoming vote count than a Texas vote?

Every state gets at least 3 electoral votes no matter how few people live there. That floor means small states end up with far more electoral power per resident than large ones — a real, measurable, and lasting gap, mapped here across every state.

The floor of 3 electoral votes does the work. The gap between the most and least "weighted" vote is roughly 4-to-1, and it comes from the rules themselves, not an accident of any particular election.

Any system that guarantees every member a minimum share hands the most weight per person to its smallest members. The US Senate (two seats per state, whatever the size) and the UN General Assembly (one vote per country) work the same way.

In the United Nations General Assembly, Tuvalu (about 10,000 people) and India (about 1.46 billion) each get one vote.

Who gains when every member is guaranteed the same minimum share?

Why small states get extra weight

Every state gets 2 electoral votes for its senators and at least 1 for a House seat, regardless of population — that floor is what gives Wyoming, Vermont, and the other smallest states outsized weight per resident. Meanwhile, the House has been capped at 435 seats by law since 1929, which keeps the total at 538 electors. As the country grows, each House seat, and so each large-state elector, has to cover more and more people.

Congressional apportionment (and electoral votes with it) is also only recalculated once per decade, on the 2020 census. States that have grown quickly since then — Texas among them — end up under-represented relative to their current population even before the small-state floor effect is considered.
