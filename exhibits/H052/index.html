Whose line is Western Sahara?

A stretch of Atlantic coastline south of Morocco appears as ordinary national territory, as an independent country, or as an unresolved question — and all three maps are actively in use today.

Morocco's position

Its Southern Provinces

Morocco administers roughly 80% of Western Sahara — the entire coastline, all major cities, and the phosphate and fishing resources — and treats it as an integral part of Moroccan territory, with no internal border drawn on official maps.

Polisario Front / SADR

An independent Sahrawi state

The Polisario Front declared the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic in 1976. It controls a strip of desert east of Morocco's defensive berm, governs from refugee camps in Algeria, and is recognized by the African Union and dozens of countries.

UN treatment

Non-self-governing territory

The UN lists Western Sahara as Africa's last unresolved decolonization case and has sought a self-determination referendum since 1991 — one that has never actually been held, over disputes about who would be allowed to vote.

A 2,700-kilometer sand wall marks the practical border no map agrees is a border. Morocco built the berm — one of the largest military-engineering projects in the world, mined and patrolled — to hold the western territory after fighting with the Polisario Front in the 1970s–80s. A 1991 ceasefire lasted until November 2020, when fighting resumed. Weeks later, the outgoing US administration recognized Moroccan sovereignty over the territory as part of a deal normalizing Morocco–Israel relations — a position most of the world, including the UN, still does not share.
Hanten Principle: Some disputes don't offer just two competing maps — they offer three, and none of them is neutral.
"Part of a country," "an independent country," and "an unresolved territory" aren't shades of the same answer. They're different legal claims, and a map has to silently choose one every time it draws a line — or doesn't.
