Does one Japanese metro area still outpopulate all of Canada?

"Greater Tokyo has more people than Canada" is one of the internet's favorite scale facts. It was true — Canada's rapid recent growth means it no longer is.

Canada's population vs. Greater Tokyo Area, 2016–2025

Canada (population) Greater Tokyo Area (range, by definition)

Canada was still below most Tokyo-area counts in 2016 (35.15M). By 2020 (38.0M) it had already passed the commonly cited "~37 million" figure — and kept climbing.

Two trends, moving in opposite directions.

Canada set successive record-high immigration targets from 2016 through 2023, pushing growth rates not seen since the 1950s. Japan, meanwhile, has had a shrinking population since the early 2010s — Tokyo's metro-area count has stayed roughly flat for a decade, and the UN's newest (2025) urban-boundary methodology puts it even lower, at 33.4 million. Put both lines on the same chart and the crossover isn't close: Canada, at about 41.5 million people, now has several million more residents than even the most generous count of Greater Tokyo.

A "fact" can be true for a decade, then quietly stop being true, while people keep repeating it.

Sources: Statistics Canada annual demographic estimates, July 1 of each year (2016: 35,151,728; 2020: 38,005,238; 2023: 40,097,761; 2024: 41,288,599; 2025: 41,651,653). Greater Tokyo Area: commonly cited "~37 million" figure per Japanese national/prefectural sources (Tokyo + Kanagawa + Saitama + Chiba); UN World Urbanization Prospects 2025 revised the Tokyo urban agglomeration figure to 33.4 million using a new, stricter density-based boundary. Ranges reflect genuinely different definitions of "Greater Tokyo," not measurement error.
