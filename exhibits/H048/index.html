Does one Japanese metro area still outpopulate all of Canada?

"Greater Tokyo has more people than Canada" is one of the internet's favorite scale facts. It was true — Canada's rapid recent growth means it no longer is.

Canada's population vs. Greater Tokyo Area, 2016–2025

Canada (population) Greater Tokyo Area (range, by definition)

Canada was still below most Tokyo-area counts in 2016 (35.15M). By 2020 (38.0M) it had already passed the commonly cited "~37 million" figure — and kept climbing.

The fact was true for years, then quietly stopped being true. Canada set successive record-high immigration targets from 2016 through 2023, pushing growth rates not seen since the 1950s. Japan, meanwhile, has had a shrinking population since the early 2010s — Tokyo's metro-area count has stayed roughly flat for a decade, and the UN's newest (2025) urban-boundary methodology puts it even lower, at 33.4 million.

Put both lines on the same chart and the crossover isn't close: Canada, at about 41.5 million people, now has several million more residents than even the most generous count of Greater Tokyo. This is what happens to any comparison built from two moving numbers: the comparison itself has a shelf life, even when neither side did anything wrong, and a factoid that was accurate for years can flip without either country's population ever behaving strangely — the crossover point was just quietly sitting there in the middle of two ordinary trend lines.

Why "Greater Tokyo" doesn't have one agreed-on number

Part of why this comparison is even debatable is that "Greater Tokyo" isn't a single administrative unit with an official population count the way a country is. The commonly cited "~37 million" figure combines Tokyo Metropolis with the neighboring Kanagawa, Saitama, and Chiba prefectures — a boundary chosen by convention, not law. The UN's 2025 World Urbanization Prospects revision applied a stricter, density-based method for defining urban agglomerations worldwide and came out with 33.4 million for Tokyo specifically — a genuinely different definition, not a correction of an error. Either way, Canada's population (a real national total, not a boundary judgment call) now sits above both figures.

Canada's numbers are Statistics Canada's official quarterly demographic estimates, updated as new immigration and birth/death data come in; they are population counts, not projections.
