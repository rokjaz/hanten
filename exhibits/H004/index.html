Is Earth’s spin carrying you at 1,000 mph?

It’s the speed everyone quotes for Earth’s rotation. How fast you’re really moving depends on where you’re standing.

How fast Earth’s spin carries you, by latitude

Speed around Earth’s axis while standing still on the ground

Every place makes one full turn in the same 23 hours 56 minutes. Near the poles, the circle is smaller.

Only near the equator. At 60° north the spin carries you half as fast, and at the North Pole it carries you nowhere at all. Every place on Earth turns once in the same 23 hours 56 minutes, but the farther you are from the equator, the smaller the circle you ride around the axis. Salt Lake City covers about 18,900 miles (30,300 km) a day; the equator covers 24,901 miles (40,075 km).

A single number for something that varies from place to place is usually the biggest case or an average, stated as if it applied everywhere. When you hear one, ask: where, and for whom?

Why don’t we feel it?

You, the ground, and the air around you all move together, so nothing rushes past you. People feel changes in motion, not steady speed, the same reason a drink stays put on a smooth flight. Earth’s spin does leave measurable traces, such as the Coriolis effect that steers large storms and ocean currents.

How the numbers are calculated

Treating Earth as a sphere, the circle you travel around the axis each turn is Earth’s equator, 24,901 miles (40,075 km), times the cosine of your latitude. Speed is that circle divided by one turn relative to the stars, 23 hours 56 minutes 4 seconds (23.9345 hours). The familiar 24-hour day is a little longer because Earth also moves along its orbit around the Sun.
