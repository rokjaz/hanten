How tall is the tallest mountain?

It depends which "tallest" you mean. Above sea level, base to summit (from the ocean floor or on land), distance from the planet's center, or how much a peak stands on its own — each definition crowns a different champion.

Above sea level

29,032 ft (8,849 m)
Mount Everest
The measurement everyone means by default — highest point above mean sea level. Nepal/China border.

Base to summit, including underwater

~33,500 ft (~10,210 m)
Mauna Kea
Measured from its true base on the Pacific Ocean floor. Only the top 13,803 ft (4,207 m) shows above water.

Base to summit, on land only

~18,000 ft (~5,500 m)
Denali
Rises from a lowland plain only about 1,000–3,000 ft (300–910 m) above sea level. Everest starts on the high Tibetan Plateau, so it rises just 12,000–15,300 ft (3,700–4,700 m) from its base.

Farthest from Earth's center

+6,800 ft (2,072 m) past Everest
Chimborazo
Sits near the equator, where the planet bulges outward — its summit reaches ~6,800 ft (2,072 m) farther into space than Everest's, despite being barely 20,549 ft (6,263 m) above sea level.

K2 is Earth's 2nd-highest peak — but it falls to 22nd in the world by prominence.

Aconcagua, Denali, and Kilimanjaro aren't even top 100 by elevation — yet they rank 2nd, 3rd, and 4th by prominence.

"Tallest mountain" isn't one fact. It's a choice of ruler, and every ruler crowns someone different. Measured by topographic prominence, how far you'd have to descend from a peak before you could climb to something taller, K2 falls to 22nd in the world, while Aconcagua, Denali, and Kilimanjaro, none of them in the top 100 by elevation, take 2nd, 3rd, and 4th.

"Longest river," "biggest country," and "richest nation" work the same way: each ranking rests on a measurement choice that is rarely stated out loud.

When you hear “tallest” or “biggest,” what definition is doing the work?

One more answer: how much a mountain stands on its own

There's one more honest measurement, and it flips the leaderboard completely: topographic prominence — roughly, how far you'd have to descend from a peak before you could climb to something taller. A mountain sitting alone on a plain scores high even if it's not very elevated; a mountain that's merely one bump on a long ridge of taller neighbors scores low, no matter how high above sea level it sits.

K2 barely counts as standalone. It's the second-highest peak on Earth by elevation, but it sits deep inside the Karakoram range, crowded with other giants — so you don't have to descend far from its summit before the surrounding terrain is already nearly as tall. That gives K2 a prominence of just 13,189 ft (4,020 m), good for only 22nd in the world.

Aconcagua, Denali, and Kilimanjaro do the opposite. None cracks the global top 100 by elevation — the Himalaya and Karakoram alone hold well over a hundred peaks above 22,966 ft (7,000 m) — yet each rises essentially alone, with no taller neighbor to borrow elevation from. That earns them the 2nd, 3rd, and 4th spots in the world by prominence.
