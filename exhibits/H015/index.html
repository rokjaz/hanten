Should you trap your enemy completely?

A complete trap sounds like a complete win. Sun Tzu advised the opposite: leave a surrounded enemy a way out. Then he gave his own army no way out at all.

For the enemy
“When you surround an army, leave an outlet free. Do not press a desperate foe too hard.”
Sun Tzu, The Art of War, VII.36
For your own army
“Throw your soldiers into positions whence there is no escape, and they will prefer death to flight.”
Sun Tzu, The Art of War, XI.23
Share of defenders who surrendered
Surrender looked like death Surrender offered as a safe way out

People with no way out have nothing to lose by fighting. On Iwo Jima in 1945, about 21,000 Japanese defenders had been taught that surrender was shameful and believed the Americans would kill them anyway. Only 216 were captured during the battle, and more than 6,800 Americans died taking the island. In the 1991 Gulf War, coalition forces dropped 29 million leaflets telling Iraqi soldiers how to surrender safely, and nearly 87,000 did. A way out doesn't have to be a gap in the line. A believable promise of safe surrender works the same way.

Sun Tzu used one idea in both directions: leave the enemy a way out so they stop fighting, and give your own soldiers none so they never stop. Whether in a war, a negotiation, or an argument, cornering the other side completely can make the fight harder, not easier.

How solid is this evidence?

These battles aren't a controlled experiment. They differ in culture, orders, how the war was going, and how hopeless each army's position was. Still, historians point to the same pattern: Japanese soldiers feared, often correctly, that Allied troops would kill them if they tried to surrender, and surrenders rose after U.S. commanders began encouraging troops to take prisoners in 1944. On Okinawa, 12% of the defenders surrendered, far more than on earlier islands.

A 2017 study of many battles by Todd Lehmann and Yuri Zhukov found that surrender spreads once it starts, and spreads faster when the opposing army has signed the Geneva Conventions, so prisoners can expect decent treatment.

A way out isn't always mercy

The Roman writer Vegetius praised the same advice, credited to the general Scipio, “that a way should be built for the enemy to flee by,” for a harder reason: “For when an escape route is revealed, the minds of all are united on turning their backs, and they are slaughtered unavenged, like sheep.” “Trapped men draw extra courage from desperation,” he wrote. “The only hope of safety for the defeated is to expect no safety.”

And closing the trap sometimes works. At Cannae in 216 BC, Hannibal surrounded a much larger Roman army and destroyed it. Leaving a way out is a trade-off: fewer of your own losses now, but some of the enemy may live to fight again.
