If a medical test is 99% accurate and you test positive, what are the chances you actually have the disease?

When the disease is rare, the answer can be nowhere near 99%.

The Base Rate Fallacy
Assume the test correctly identifies 99% of sick people and correctly clears 99% of healthy people. Only 1 in 1,000 people has the disease. You test positive. What is the chance you actually have it?

A 99% accurate test does not mean a positive result is 99% likely to be correct. When only 1 in 1,000 people has the disease, this example gives a positive result only about a 9% chance of being a true positive.

Before asking how strong the new evidence is, how likely was the thing you were testing for in the first place?

Why does 99% accuracy become about 9%?

Imagine 1,000 people. About one actually has the disease, and a 99% sensitive test will usually find that person. But about 999 people are healthy. A 1% false-positive rate incorrectly flags roughly ten of them. That leaves about one true positive among roughly eleven positive results.

Source basis preserved from the original exhibit: Bayes’ theorem; Kahneman & Tversky (1974) on base-rate neglect; Gigerenzer & Hoffrage (1995) on probabilistic reasoning in medical-test contexts. The interactive example assumes equal sensitivity and specificity.
