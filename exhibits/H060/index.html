The NFL fixed the coin-toss advantage in overtime. Did it stay fixed?

In 2010, the NFL changed its overtime format specifically because winning the coin toss was too big an advantage. The fix worked for a few years — then a separate, unrelated rule change quietly undid it.

A perfectly fair coin toss would win the game exactly 50% of the time, on average, no matter who calls it.

By 2024, winning the coin toss was a bigger advantage than it was before the league tried to fix it. The 2010–2012 rule change cut the coin-toss winner's edge to a near-even 51.2%. Then a 2017 change aimed at player safety shortened overtime from 15 minutes to 10, leaving the second team less time to answer, and the coin-toss winner's rate climbed to 59.7%.

A fix only holds while the conditions it was built for hold. When a rule changes for an unrelated reason, check whether it quietly undoes an earlier fix; a solved problem can come back without anyone deciding it should.

When a rule changes for one reason, what else might it quietly change?

Shortening the clock brought the imbalance back

By 2024, winning the coin toss was a bigger advantage than it was before the league ever tried to fix it. Under sudden death (2005–2011), whoever scored first won, and a coin-toss-winning offense often only needed a field goal. The league fixed this in 2010–2012: the second team now gets a possession unless the first team scores a touchdown. It worked — the coin-toss winner's win rate dropped to a near-even 51.2% from 2012–2016.

Then, in 2017, the league shortened overtime from 15 minutes to 10 — a change aimed at player safety, unrelated to fairness. With less time on the clock, the second team gets fewer chances to answer a scoring drive. From 2017–2024, the coin-toss winner's win rate climbed to 59.7% — worse than the original sudden-death era the 2010 rule was designed to fix. In the playoffs under the post-2010 rule, coin-toss winners went 10–1 before the NFL made a further postseason-only tweak in 2022.
