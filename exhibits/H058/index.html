Do your friends really have more friends than you?

This isn't about being unpopular. It's a near-universal property of social networks, measured directly on the real Facebook graph at the scale of hundreds of millions of accounts.

Facebook's social graph, ~721 million active users (Ugander et al., 2011)

92.7%
of users have fewer friends than their friends' average
83.6%
have fewer friends than their friends' median

Only people in the top ~10% of friend counts (over 780 friends, in that dataset) tended to have friends with smaller networks than their own, on average.

Why: a 300-person simulated network, built live, with no popularity rule

Computing…

Popular people count more times — once per friend they have

Build a network where new connections are just a little more likely to attach to already-well-connected people — the same simple rule that produces real social networks, airline route maps, and citation networks. No node "tries" to be popular; the rule never mentions popularity at all.

Now average, for each person, how many friends their friends have. A person with 200 friends gets counted in that average 200 times — once in every friend list they belong to. A person with 3 friends gets counted only 3 times. The well-connected minority dominates everyone else's average, which pulls almost everybody's "my friends' average" above their own friend count.

It is mathematically guaranteed that most people in almost any social network have friends who are, on average, more connected than they are — no bias, no illusion, just arithmetic.

When a sample is drawn "one friend at a time," is it a random sample of people — or a sample weighted by how connected they are?

Sources: Johan Ugander, Brian Karrer, Lars Backstrom, Cameron Marlow, "The Anatomy of the Facebook Social Graph," arXiv:1111.4503 (2011), analyzing the full active Facebook user graph at the time (~721 million users). Phenomenon first described by Scott L. Feld, "Why Your Friends Have More Friends Than You Do," American Journal of Sociology, 1991. The scatter plot above is generated live in your browser from a seeded random simulation (Barabási–Albert preferential-attachment network, 300 nodes) — not the real Facebook data, but the same structural mechanism.
