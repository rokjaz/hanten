If 94% of published studies say a drug works, is the question settled?

Every published result can be real, and the pile of them can still mislead. Studies that find something get printed; studies that find little often don't, or get written up as if they had.

What gets published isn't a fair sample of what was tested. Almost every trial that worked made it into a journal. Most of the ones that didn't either stayed in a drawer or were written up to sound like successes. A doctor reading every published paper would see a drug that almost always works, when the full record was closer to a coin flip.

The same filter works anywhere only the hits get shown: investing newsletters that advertise their winning picks, diets that only share success stories, a highlight reel of a season. Before trusting a stack of positive results, ask what didn't get shown.

The drugs still beat placebo overall. But in the published papers their effect looked about a third bigger (32%) than in the FDA's complete data. Today, U.S. law requires most clinical trials to be registered before they start and to report results, which makes missing trials easier to spot.
