What's the biggest country?

"Biggest" seems like the one geography superlative that shouldn't need a definition. But depending on whether you count water, and how much of the land is actually farmable, the ranking changes twice — and Canada, the textbook #2, ends up nowhere near the top.

Three rankings, three different runners-up

Every bar above starts at the country's full claimed territory (the light track, always 100%). The tick mark shows how much is dry land once inland water is subtracted. The teal bar shows how much of the original territory is actually arable — land that could grow crops, the roughest available proxy for "usable."

Canada's slide isn't a data quirk — it's two real geographic facts compounding. About 9% of Canada's total area is fresh water (it holds more lakes than the rest of the world's countries combined), which alone drops it from 2nd to 4th once you count land only. Most of what's left is boreal forest, tundra, or Arctic permafrost — only about 4.3% of Canada's land is arable, one of the lowest shares of any large country. The United States, by contrast, has plenty of temperate, non-mountainous land suited to agriculture: over 16% of its land is arable, more than triple Canada's share — enough to make the 3rd-largest country by total area the single largest by usable farmland.

Canada is the world's 2nd-biggest country by total area — and only its 5th-biggest by farmable land.
