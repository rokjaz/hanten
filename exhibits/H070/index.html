Total emissions and per-person emissions answer two different questions. Totals measure how much CO₂ a country adds to the atmosphere; per-person figures measure how carbon-intensive a typical resident's life is. India is 3rd in the world by total but under half the global average per person, while Saudi Arabia and Australia rank 6th and 10th by total among these ten, and 1st and 2nd per person.

Citing only one of the two numbers can make the same country sound uniquely responsible or barely involved. Any comparison of places with very different populations, whether crime, spending, or energy use, needs both the total and the rate.

Two different questions, two different leaderboards

Total emissions answer a physical question: how much CO₂ did a country add to the atmosphere this year? That's the number that matters for the climate, since the atmosphere doesn't care how many people produced it. Per-person emissions answer a different question: how carbon-intensive is the typical resident's life, regardless of how many residents there are. A country can top one list and sit near the bottom of the other, and neither number is wrong.

India is the sharpest example. Its total is enormous, 3rd in the world, mostly because it has more people than any other country. Divide by that population and each resident's share is under half the global average. Citing only the total makes India sound uniquely responsible; citing only the per-person figure makes its impact vanish. Both together are the honest picture.

Saudi Arabia and Australia sit at the opposite extreme: 6th and 10th by total among these ten, but 1st and 2nd per person. They're wealthy, energy-intensive economies where high individual use isn't diluted by a huge population.
