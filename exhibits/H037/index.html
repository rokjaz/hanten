Whose country is Crimea on the map?

The same peninsula can be drawn three different ways, depending on what the boundary is meant to show.

As shown in Ukraine
Common international treatment
Russian-map treatment

Fully integrated

Russian government maps show Crimea with the same solid, ordinary border as any other Russian region — nothing marks it as different or contested.

Ukrainian-map treatment

Part of Ukraine

Ukrainian maps draw no line separating Crimea from the rest of the country — it's simply Ukraine, the same as any other oblast, occupied or not.

International-map treatment

Dashed or disputed

Most international map providers draw a distinct dashed border, acknowledging Russia's on-the-ground control since 2014 without endorsing the annexation.

The coastline does not change. The meaning of the boundary does. Russia annexed Crimea in March 2014, days after Russian forces took control of the peninsula and a disputed referendum was held under military occupation. The UN General Assembly responded with Resolution 68/262, affirming Ukraine's territorial integrity within its internationally recognized borders, including Crimea — and the vast majority of the world's governments still do not recognize the annexation as legitimate.

A map has to draw every border one way or another, so even a thin dashed line takes a position. Whenever a map shows a disputed place, ask whose view it's drawn from.

What is the line on your map actually claiming?

Explore how the annexation happened, and how this pattern shows up elsewhere

Russian forces took control of the peninsula in late February and early March 2014, and a referendum on joining Russia was held under that occupation days later — a vote most of the world does not recognize as free or legitimate. Russia formally annexed Crimea on March 18, 2014.

In practice, that leaves cartographers picking a side just by drawing a line: Russian state maps show no distinction at all, Ukrainian maps show no distinction either, in the opposite direction, and everyone else tends to split the difference with a dashed or dotted boundary — the standard cartographic signal for "administered by one party, claimed by another." The same signal shows up in the Senkaku, Diaoyu, or Diaoyutai Islands, where administration and recognized sovereignty likewise point in different directions — a map's line can show legal recognition, a territorial claim, or control on the ground, and rarely all three at once. The recognition count tells its own version of the same story: Northern Cyprus is recognized as a country by exactly one government, Turkey — the same one whose army created it in 1974, much as Crimea's annexation is recognized by only a handful of Russia-aligned states. Even the "neutral" option isn't really neutral: a dashed line still defaults to the boundary the UN and most governments recognize, quietly siding with Ukraine's claim over Russia's control, just less loudly than a solid line would. There's no version of this map that takes no position — only versions that are more or less obvious about which position they've taken.
