How Far Apart Are a Million and a Billion, Really?

A billion is not a bigger million — it is a thousand millions. A trillion is a thousand billions.

In time: a vacation, a generation, an ice age

There are 86,400 seconds in a day. One million seconds is 11.57 days — about the length of a two-week trip. One billion seconds is 31.69 years — essentially a generation, the age gap between a parent and a child. One trillion seconds is 31,688 years — a span that reaches back past the Last Glacial Maximum, the coldest point of the last Ice Age (26,000–20,000 years ago), and nearly three times further back than the start of the current geological epoch, the Holocene, which began only about 11,700 years ago. Each step is a clean ×1,000. None of them feel like the same-sized step.

In money: a countertop, a skyline, low orbit

A U.S. banknote is 0.0043 inches thick, regardless of denomination. Stack ten thousand $100 bills — one million dollars — and the pile is 1.09 meters tall, about counter height. A billion dollars in the same bills is 1,092 meters tall: taller than the Burj Khalifa, the world's tallest building, by more than 260 meters. A trillion dollars is 1,092 kilometers tall — 2.7 times the altitude at which the International Space Station orbits Earth. Two multiplications by 1,000 carry the stack from "furniture" to "skyscraper" to "past low Earth orbit."

In space: shrink the Sun to a basketball, and the nearest star is still 6,930 km away

Shrink the Sun from its true diameter (1,391,400 km) down to a 24 cm basketball, and every other distance in the solar system shrinks by the same ratio: about 1 part in 5.8 billion. At that scale, Earth is a 2.2 mm speck — a sesame seed — orbiting 25.8 meters away, roughly the length of a basketball court. Neptune, the outermost planet, sits about 776 meters out: seven or eight football fields. The whole solar system fits in a few city blocks. Proxima Centauri, the single nearest star to the Sun, is 4.2465 light-years away in reality. Shrunk by the same 5.8-billion-to-one ratio, it still lands 6,930 kilometers from the basketball — about 69 hours of nonstop highway driving, on a scale where the rest of the solar system was already a rounding error.

Compressing the Sun by 5.8 billion times was an extreme shrink by any household standard, and it still wasn't nearly enough to bring interstellar space down to a human scale.
