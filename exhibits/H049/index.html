Can a single Indian state outpopulate most countries on Earth?

Uttar Pradesh isn't a country — it's one of India's 28 states. Line it up against actual countries by population and it lands in the same league as Pakistan, ahead of Nigeria, Brazil, and Japan.

China and India themselves are off this chart entirely — at 1.4 billion each, they'd stretch it more than five times wider.

"Most populous country" and "most populous place" aren't the same list. Uttar Pradesh, India's most populous state, holds an estimated 243 million people — more than Nigeria, Brazil, Bangladesh, Russia, Mexico, or Japan, each counted as a whole country. Guangdong, China's most populous province, holds about 128 million — more than Japan, a G7 economy, on its own.

Both provinces are administrative subdivisions of countries so large that their internal pieces rival entire nations elsewhere. Uttar Pradesh isn't even a special case — several other Indian states would also crack the world's top twenty countries by population if they were counted as nations. "Biggest" only means something once you've agreed on the unit.

Why India and China have provinces this large

Uttar Pradesh and Guangdong aren't unusually large administrative units by area — India has 28 states and China has 23 provinces, so each one is, on average, a modest slice of a very large country. What makes them enormous in population is that both countries are themselves the two most populous nations on Earth, each above 1.4 billion, and both have historically dense agricultural river-valley regions (the Ganges plain for Uttar Pradesh, the Pearl River Delta for Guangdong) that concentrated population growth for centuries before either country existed in its current form. Divide 1.4 billion people among a few dozen states or provinces and even an average-sized one lands in the hundreds of millions.

Population figures for provinces and countries alike come from different national statistical agencies with different methodologies and reference dates, which is why the source note below flags Uttar Pradesh and Pakistan specifically as close enough to swap places depending on which year's estimate is used.

It's the flip side of the Tokyo-versus-Canada comparison: there, a metro area and a country turned out to be closer in size than expected; here, a country's internal subdivision turns out to be bigger than most whole countries. Same lesson either way.

It is the same population-concentration pattern as our Half of the World Lives Here exhibit, just contained inside one country’s internal borders instead of a circle on a globe: centuries of dense agricultural settlement can pack more people into a single administrative region than live in most entire nations.
