Where should you armor the bomber — where the bullet holes are, or where they aren't?

In 1943, engineers mapped the damage on bombers that made it home. A statistician named Abraham Wald looked at the same map and reached the opposite conclusion.

The missing data was the whole answer

Every plane the military could inspect had already survived its damage. A hole in the wing or tail was evidence that a plane could take a hit there and still fly home. The spots with almost no holes — the engines, the cockpit, the fuel system — weren't lucky. Planes hit there went down and never made it back to be counted.

Wald, working for Columbia's Statistical Research Group, told the Navy to armor the undamaged areas on returning planes, not the damaged ones. His recommendation was adopted and is credited with saving many aircrews.

The safest-looking spots on the chart were actually the most dangerous ones — because a dataset built entirely from survivors can't show you what killed everyone else.
