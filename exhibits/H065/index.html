Why the same country can top one list and vanish from another

Total GDP just adds up everyone's output — mostly a function of population size multiplied by productivity, which is why the U.S. and China dominate it regardless of how wealthy any individual resident is. GDP per capita divides that output by population, which flips the list toward small, specialized economies — financial hubs, city-states, and countries where a small population divides a disproportionately large output. None of the total-GDP top five shows up here.

Median wealth per adult asks a third, different question: what does the person exactly in the middle of the distribution actually own? This one punishes concentration. Switzerland has the world's highest average wealth per adult ($910,000) — but its median is only $146,000, because a relatively small number of extremely wealthy residents pull the average far above what a typical Swiss adult holds. The United States shows the same pattern even more starkly: it ranks 2nd in the world by average wealth per adult, but falls all the way to 28th when ranked by median — one of the largest average-to-median drops of any country.

Only Luxembourg shows up in more than one of these lists — 2nd by GDP per capita and 1st by median wealth. The United States, the clear #1 by total GDP, doesn't crack the top five on either of the other two measures.

When someone says a country is "rich," which of these three questions are they actually answering?
