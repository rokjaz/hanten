Falkland Islands, or Islas Malvinas?

A cluster of islands 300 miles off Argentina's coast is drawn as unambiguously British, unambiguously Argentine, or carefully hedged — depending entirely on whose map you're reading.

Common international treatment
UK position

Falkland Islands, British since 1833

The UK has continuously administered the islands since 1833. It points to self-determination: in a 2013 referendum, Falkland Islanders voted 99.8% (1,513 to 3, on 92% turnout) to remain a British Overseas Territory.

Argentina's position

Islas Malvinas, Argentine territory

Argentina claims sovereignty inherited from Spain and asserts the islands were seized by Britain in 1833. Its constitution names recovering "the Malvinas" a permanent national goal, and Argentine maps show them as part of Tierra del Fuego Province.

International treatment

Both names, disputed status noted

The UN lists the islands as a Non-Self-Governing Territory and has repeatedly called on the UK and Argentina to negotiate sovereignty — a call the UK rejects, citing the islanders' own vote.

The dispute escalated into a war, not just a labeling disagreement. Argentina invaded the islands on 2 April 1982; Britain retook them after a 74-day conflict, ending 14 June 1982. The war killed 649 Argentine service members, 255 British service members, and 3 Falkland Islanders. More than 40 years later, both governments still maintain their original claims, and the islands still appear differently depending on whose map you open.
Hanten Principle: "Whose is it?" can have two internally consistent answers at once.
Proximity, history, and the preference of the people who actually live there don't always point to the same conclusion — and a map has to pick one anyway.
