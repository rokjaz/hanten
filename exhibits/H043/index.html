Is Reno, Nevada west of Los Angeles?

Reno reads as an "east of California" city. By longitude it isn't — draw a true north–south line through downtown Los Angeles, and Reno falls on the ocean side of it.

LA's line of longitude Reno, NV Los Angeles, CA

California’s coast leans east, so Los Angeles ends up east of Reno. California's coast isn't a vertical line — it leans. The northern border sits near 124.2°W; by San Diego the coast has drifted to about 117.2°W, nearly seven degrees east over roughly 800 miles of shoreline. The hinge is around Point Conception, near Santa Barbara: north of it, the coast runs roughly northwest–southeast as expected; south of it, the coast — and Los Angeles with it — swings sharply east. Reno sits in the less-drifted northern zone, just inside Nevada by Lake Tahoe, so it ends up west of a city that reads, on a mental map, as clearly "in California."

Mental maps simplify: we remember states as boxes and coasts as straight lines. When a claim about direction feels obvious, check the coordinates rather than the shape in your head.

It isn't only Reno. San Francisco, on the undrifted northern coast, is a full 228 miles west of LA's meridian — no surprise there. But San Diego, another 120 miles down the coast from LA, is actually about 63 miles east of it. And Las Vegas, which most people mentally file next to Reno, sits roughly 174 miles east of LA's meridian — more than three degrees of longitude from its fellow Nevada city. "East" and "west" feel like fixed facts about a place, but they're really just a comparison against a reference line — and a mental map built from a bent coastline will get that comparison backwards without ever being wrong about anything else. The same failure shows up on the other axis, too: London sits north of nearly all of Canada's population, for the same reason — nobody checks the coordinates against the mental map until something forces the comparison.

Why the coast bends

The hinge is Point Conception and the Transverse Ranges, an unusual east–west-trending mountain belt near Santa Barbara — one of the only stretches where the coast briefly runs sideways instead of its usual northwest–southeast line. South of that hinge, the coastline folds inland, tucking the entire Los Angeles Basin east relative to the rest of the state.
