Choose a ruler length

Each ruler is drawn to the same scale as the map.

There's no ruler length short enough to make the measurement stop growing. A coastline is jagged at every scale. Measure it with a 50-mile (80 km) ruler and you walk straight across inlets; switch to a 10-mile (16 km) ruler and you catch bends the longer ruler missed, so the total goes up.

Any number measured at a chosen level of detail works the same way. Two countries' published coastline lengths are only comparable if both were measured at the same resolution, so when a figure depends on how closely you look, ask what scale it was measured at.

Why the number never settles

Real coastlines behave the same way. Measured with a 62-mile (100 km) ruler, Great Britain's coastline comes out to about 1,740 miles (2,800 km); with a 31-mile (50 km) ruler, about 2,110 miles (3,400 km).

Mathematicians call this a coastline's fractal dimension: Great Britain's west coast measures at roughly 1.25 (a straight line would be 1.0; a shape so wrinkled it starts filling a whole area would approach 2.0). This is also why national "coastline length" rankings are shakier than they look — two countries' published figures are only comparable if both were measured at the same resolution, and in practice that's often not true.
