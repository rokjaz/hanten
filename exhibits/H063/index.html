Why the number never settles

A coastline is jagged at every scale — bays inside bays, headlands inside headlands, down past what any map can show. Measure it with a 200 km ruler and you walk straight across small inlets smaller than that; switch to a 50 km ruler and you catch bends the longer ruler missed, so the total goes up. Every time you halve the ruler, you catch another layer of detail the previous ruler was too coarse to see.

Mathematicians call this a coastline's fractal dimension: Great Britain's west coast measures at roughly 1.25 (a straight line would be 1.0; a shape so wrinkled it starts filling a whole area would approach 2.0). This is also why national "coastline length" rankings are shakier than they look — two countries' published figures are only comparable if both were measured at the same resolution, and in practice that's often not true.

There's no ruler length short enough to make the measurement stop growing.
