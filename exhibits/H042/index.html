How can two islands 2.4 miles apart be a day apart?

Big Diomede (Russia) and Little Diomede (USA) sit close enough in the Bering Strait to see each other on a clear day. The International Date Line runs through the water between them.

2.4 miles of water, and the clocks read 20 or 21 hours apart, never a clean 24. Big Diomede runs on Kamchatka Time, a fixed UTC+12 that Russia's Chukotka region has held year-round since Russia abolished daylight saving nationally in 2014. Little Diomede runs on Alaska Time, UTC−9 for most of the year and UTC−8 during daylight-saving months. Twelve minus negative nine is twenty-one; twelve minus negative eight is twenty — so the gap swings between 21 and 20 hours, always shy of a full day, with Big Diomede always further along the calendar.

A line drawn for convenience can open a gap far bigger than the distance it crosses. When two nearby places disagree about something as basic as the date, look at the rules each side chose, not the miles between them.

The asymmetry runs deeper than the clocks. Little Diomede is home to a real, if tiny, community — 77 people as of 2023, an Iñupiat village with a school, a store, and a runway used mostly by small planes. Big Diomede has no permanent civilian population at all; the island hosts only a Russian weather station and a border post, its native Iñupiat community relocated to the Russian mainland decades ago during the Cold War, when the strait itself briefly earned the nickname the "Ice Curtain." The maritime border near the date line here is also the line the United States and the Soviet Union agreed on in a 1990 treaty, one of the last pieces of Cold War-era boundary-making still quietly doing its job.

In 1987, American swimmer Lynne Cox actually crossed the gap between them, covering about 2.2 miles of frigid water as a piece of Cold War diplomacy — an American touching Soviet soil by the most literal means available. Distance and time, it turns out, are governed by entirely separate sets of rules: geography put these islands within shouting distance of each other, while history and time-zone politics put them on different calendar pages for all but a sliver of the day. Farther south along the same 180th meridian, the line stops dividing calendar days and starts dividing east from west instead, at the far tip of Alaska's Aleutian Islands — one line of longitude, doing two entirely different jobs depending on where you cross it.
