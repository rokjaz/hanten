Somaliland has run its own government since 1991. Is it a country?

It depends on who you ask. Recognizing a country, or deciding whose territory a place is, is a choice each government makes for itself. So there isn't one world map; there's a different one for almost every foreign ministry.

A border on a map can be a fact on the ground and a matter of opinion at the same time. Somaliland runs its own affairs, but whether it's a country depends on which government you ask. No row in the table is unanimous, and no two of these powers agree on every row: the U.S. is almost alone on Western Sahara, Russia on Crimea, and the world splits on Kosovo.

The same is true of names, holidays and even time zones: things that feel settled are often choices that different authorities make differently. When a map, list or label looks official, ask who drew it, and whether someone else would draw it another way.

More on each dispute

Taiwan: 12 governments have formal ties with Taiwan; 11 are UN members (the 12th is the Holy See). The U.S. doesn't formally recognize it but keeps close unofficial ties.

Kosovo: declared independence from Serbia in 2008. Kosovo counts more recognitions than Serbia does, because Serbia counts countries it says have withdrawn theirs.

Western Sahara: the U.S. recognized Moroccan sovereignty in 2020 and Israel in 2023. In 2025 the UN Security Council backed Morocco's autonomy plan as a basis for talks; Russia and China abstained.

Northern Cyprus: declared independence in 1983; only Turkey recognizes it.

Crimea: annexed by Russia in 2014. That year the UN General Assembly declared the annexation vote invalid, 100 votes to 11, with 58 abstaining.

Somaliland: declared independence from Somalia in 1991. Israel became the first UN member to recognize it, in December 2025; the U.S. still backs a single Somalia.

Some online maps even change the borders they show depending on which country you view them from.

Western Sahara: a 1,700-mile wall no map calls a border

Three maps, three different legal claims. Morocco’s maps show its “Southern Provinces” with no internal border. The Polisario Front, which declared the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic in 1976, shows an independent state recognized by the African Union and dozens of countries. The UN lists the territory as Africa’s last unresolved decolonization case.

The berm. Morocco controls roughly 80% of the territory, including the whole coastline and every major city, behind a sand wall about 1,700 miles (2,700 km) long, roughly New York to Denver, lined with millions of landmines. A UN mission was set up in 1991 to hold a referendum on the territory’s future; the vote has never happened, stalled over who counts as an eligible voter. A ceasefire held from 1991 until fighting resumed in November 2020.

Northern Cyprus: a line through the capital

One island, one or two countries depending on the map. The Republic of Cyprus, an EU member, claims the whole island and treats the north as occupied since Turkey’s 1974 invasion. The Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus declared independence in 1983; only Turkey recognizes it, and UN Security Council resolutions call the declaration legally invalid.

The Green Line. A UN-patrolled buffer zone runs about 112 miles (180 km) across the island and straight through the capital, Nicosia, where it narrows to a few yards between checkpoints. Crossing points didn’t open until 2003. A UN reunification plan went to a vote in 2004: Turkish Cypriots approved it, Greek Cypriots rejected it, and the island stayed divided. Cyprus joined the EU days later, but EU law applies only in the south.
