One island. How many countries?

Cyprus's capital, Nicosia, is one of the last divided cities on Earth. Depending on whose map you open, the island north of that line is occupied territory, a buffer zone, or a country in its own right.

Republic of Cyprus

One country, occupied in part

The internationally recognized Republic of Cyprus — an EU member since 2004 — claims the entire island. It considers the north to be Cypriot territory under Turkish military occupation since 1974, not a separate state.

Turkey / TRNC

Two states on one island

The Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus declared independence in 1983. Turkey is the only UN member state that recognizes it, maintains roughly 30,000 troops there, and treats it as a separate country on its own maps.

UN treatment

One state, a buffer zone, and a warning

UN Security Council resolutions declared the TRNC's 1983 declaration legally invalid and call on all states not to recognize it. A UN-patrolled buffer zone — the Green Line — physically divides the island, including the capital, Nicosia.

The line runs straight through the capital. In 1974, a Greek Cypriot coup aiming for union with Greece triggered a Turkish military invasion. By the ceasefire, Turkish forces held about 37% of the island. Tens of thousands on both sides were displaced across the new line. Fifty years on, Nicosia remains split by checkpoints and a UN buffer zone — a divided capital in a way most of the world last saw in Cold War Berlin.
Hanten Principle: "One country" and "two countries" can both be someone's accurate map.
Military control, legal recognition, and a population's day-to-day reality don't automatically agree — and each version of the map is drawn by people for whom their version isn't a simplification, it's the fact.
