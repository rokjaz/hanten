Is South America east of Florida?

Most people picture South America hanging directly below the United States. Draw a straight north–south line down from Pensacola, on the Florida Panhandle, and the entire South American mainland lands east of it.

Pensacola's longitude (87.2°W) Punta Pariñas' longitude (81.3°W)

All of mainland South America lies east of Pensacola, even its westernmost point. Punta Pariñas, Peru, sits at about 81.3°W, still about 6° east of Pensacola's 87.2°W. The one catch is which part of Florida you pick: Miami, at 80.2°W, is about 1° east of Peru's westernmost point.

Mental maps stack places in neat columns that the real globe doesn't follow. North America sits well to the northwest and South America bulges sharply east, so the two continents are arranged more like a staircase than a straight line. When a direction feels obvious, check the coordinates.

It holds even at the continent's westernmost point

The hardest test is South America's own westernmost point. That's Punta Pariñas, on Peru's Pacific coast, at about 81.3°W. Pensacola sits at about 87.2°W. In west longitudes a smaller number means farther east, so even the single closest sliver of South American land is still about 6° east of Pensacola — roughly 410 miles (660 km) at that latitude. The claim holds for the whole continent, not just "most of it." (The one exception is offshore: Ecuador's Galápagos Islands, about 560 miles (906 km) out in the Pacific at roughly 90°W, sit just west of the line — the small specks left of it on the map.)

The catch: it depends which part of Florida you pick. Most of Florida — Pensacola, Tallahassee, Tampa, even Jacksonville (81.7°W) — lies west of Punta Pariñas, so all of South America is east of them. But Florida's southeast coast reaches past that line: Miami sits at 80.2°W, about 1° east of Peru's westernmost point. From Miami, a sliver of South America is technically to the west.

Why it defies intuition: the Americas aren't stacked in a neat north–south column. North America sits well to the northwest, while South America bulges sharply east — its easternmost tip near Recife, Brazil, is closer to West Africa than to Florida. The two continents are arranged more like a staircase than a straight line.
